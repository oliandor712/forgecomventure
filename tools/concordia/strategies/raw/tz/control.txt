Control: The Commander Archetype, Explained
Control decks answer things. The hard part in Commander is that there are three times as many things to answer, and only one of you.

Control: Answers everything and wins late, once the table has run out of threats.
What Control Decks Actually Do
A control deck aims to neutralise every threat that matters until it can deploy a win condition safely. Counterspells, removal, board wipes, and card draw make up most of the list, and the actual way it wins is often two or three cards deep in a ninety-nine-card deck.
Multiplayer changes the archetype more than any other. In a duel, one-for-one answers are fine because there is one opponent; in Commander a Counterspell trades with one card out of three opponents' hands, and you fall behind by answering everything. Successful control decks therefore lean on effects that answer many things at once (board wipes, mass bounce, taxation) and on card draw that keeps up with three players drawing at the same time.
The Control engine
Three steps that feed each other, which is what makes it a deck rather than a pile of cards sharing a theme.
1 Hold mana open Untapped mana is the threat, whether or not you have the card.
2 Answer the threat Counterspell on the stack, or Swords to Plowshares after it lands.
3 Untap They spent a card and a turn; you spent a card.
↻ Win late, once three opponents have run out of things to try.
Which Colors Fit Control
Blue is the archetype, effectively by definition: counterspells and the deepest card draw both live there. White supplies the board wipes and the cheapest unconditional removal, and black supplies targeted removal and its own sweepers. Red and green participate least: red's answers are damage-based and narrow, and green's are one-for-one artifact and enchantment removal rather than broad control tools.
Blue Counterspells, card draw, and mass bounce. The color the archetype cannot exist without.
White Board wipes and the most efficient unconditional removal in the format. Answers many threats at once, which is what multiplayer demands.
Black Targeted removal that hits anything, plus sweepers that get around indestructible.
Red Narrow support: artifact removal and damage-based sweepers. Rarely a core control color.
Green The least suited. Green answers artifacts and enchantments well and almost nothing else on the control axis.

Top 10 Control Cards
The cards that define the archetype rather than the ones winning this week. If you are
building control for the first time, these are the effects to
understand before anything else.
Counterspell ↗
Two mana, no conditions. Still the baseline every other counterspell is measured against.
Cyclonic Rift ↗
Overloaded, it returns every nonland permanent your opponents control at instant speed. The single most game-swinging card in the archetype.
Swords to Plowshares ↗
One mana to exile any creature. The life gained is close to irrelevant at forty.
Wrath of God ↗
Four mana, all creatures, no regeneration. Answering three players' boards with one card is how control keeps pace.
Toxic Deluge ↗
A scaling sweeper paid for in life, which gets around indestructible and large creatures alike.
Rhystic Study ↗
Taxes every spell the table casts, and either draws you cards or slows everyone down. Both outcomes are what control wants.
Mystic Remora ↗
Draws enormously in the early turns for one mana, then retires. The best cheap draw engine in multiplayer.
Fierce Guardianship ↗
A free counterspell with your commander out, which lets you tap out on your own turn and still hold up interaction.
Farewell ↗
Modal exile of any combination of artifacts, creatures, enchantments, and graveyards. One card that answers whole strategies.
Nevinyrral's Disk ↗
A repeatable-threat sweeper that hits artifacts and enchantments too, and is available in any color.
Top 10 Control Commanders
Ten command zones that support the strategy, spread across the color combinations it lives
in. The color identity shown is what each commander lets you play in the other 99.
Commander Spotlight
Baral, Chief of Compliance ↗
Cheaper counterspells and loot on every counter. Mono-blue control with the draw problem partly solved.
Teferi, Temporal Archmage ↗
A planeswalker commander that untaps four permanents each turn, so you counter on their turn and still develop on yours.
Azor, the Lawbringer ↗
A sphinx that draws a large chunk of a deck and stops opponents casting instants and sorceries for a turn.
Dragonlord Ojutai ↗
Hexproof while untapped and dig on damage. A control finisher that survives everything a control deck fears.
Dragonlord Silumgar ↗
Steals a creature or planeswalker and keeps stealing as he is recast. Removal that becomes your threat.
The Scarab God ↗
Scry and drain each upkeep, exiles graveyards for Zombies, and returns to hand instead of dying. Nearly unanswerable.
Ertai Resurrected ↗
Flash plus a modal counter or removal on entry, and he returns to the command zone rather than dying.
Rashmi, Eternities Crafter ↗
Free spells off the top on your first spell each turn, which fixes control's card-advantage math.
Nymris, Oona's Trickster ↗
Draws whenever you cast on an opponent's turn, exactly the play pattern control already wants.
Damia, Sage of Stone ↗
Refills you to seven cards every upkeep, which makes trading one-for-one with three opponents sustainable.

What to Watch Out For
The archetype's real risk is being the table's police officer. If you counter everything, three players learn that you are the problem, and in a format where they can all attack you at once that is a losing position regardless of how good your answers are. Let some things resolve, save your counterspells for what actually beats you, and make sure the deck can close a game rather than only prolong it.
Frequently Asked Questions
Is control good in multiplayer Commander?
It works, but it needs building differently than a duel control deck. One-for-one answers fall behind against three opponents, so multiplayer control leans on board wipes, mass bounce, and taxation effects that answer several players at once, plus card draw that keeps pace with a whole table.
What colors are control decks in Commander?
Blue above all, for counterspells and card draw. White adds board wipes and efficient removal, black adds unconditional targeted removal. Azorius, Dimir, and Esper are the classic control color combinations.
How does a control deck actually win?
Usually with a small number of resilient threats deployed once the board is stable: a hexproof commander, a large flier, or an alternate win condition. Some control decks win by decking opponents or with a single combo instead, since the deck already plays the interaction needed to protect it.
